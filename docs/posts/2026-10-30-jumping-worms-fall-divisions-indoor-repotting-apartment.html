Potting Up Fall Plant Divisions to Bring Indoors? Invasive Jumping Worms Make This the Riskiest Week to Skip Fresh Soil

2026-10-30

With freeze warnings having swept through one region after another this October, a lot of apartment growers are doing the same thing right now: digging up a few divisions, cuttings, or clearance perennials from an outdoor bed or a neighbor's garden and potting them up to overwinter on a windowsill. Extension offices are flagging this exact week as the highest-risk moment for spreading invasive jumping worms (Amynthas species), because fall is when the worms are largest and easiest to notice in the soil — but also when they've likely already laid the tiny, soil-colored cocoons that are nearly impossible to see. Skip the soil check this week and you're not just risking your potted plant; you could be moving a pest problem into the one place winter normally keeps it in check.

What you're actually looking for before you pot anything up

Jumping worms get their name from how violently they thrash when handled — University of Vermont and University of Illinois Extension both describe the behavior as closer to a snake or a severed wire than a typical earthworm. The bigger tell is the soil itself: heavily infested soil takes on a loose, grainy, coffee-grounds texture because the worms consume organic matter so fast they strip out its structure. If a garden bed or a nursery pot you're dividing from has that texture, treat it as a red flag before you ever touch a trowel to it. The harder problem is the cocoons — about 1-2 millimeters, colored like a fleck of soil, and essentially invisible in a scoop of dirt. Extension sources are consistent that by the time adult worms are big and obvious in fall, cocoons may already be present in that same soil.

Why bringing that soil indoors doesn't 'reset' anything the way winter does outdoors

Outdoors, jumping worm adults die off once the cold really sets in, and the population has to rebuild each year from cocoons that survive winter dormant in the ground and hatch the following spring — the freeze is at least an annual pause. A houseplant pot sitting at normal apartment temperature skips that pause entirely. Heat is what extension researchers actually point to for killing cocoons, not cold: sources from University of Vermont and University of Wisconsin Arboretum researchers put the lethal threshold around 104°F, with a Minnesota nursery-industry group citing roughly 115°F for cocoons versus about 90°F for active worms. A typical apartment sits at 65-75°F — nowhere near that range, and also nowhere near freezing. So soil that would have gotten a hard reset left outside can instead just sit in a warm pot through the winter, with nothing stopping a cocoon from hatching on its own schedule.

The actual prevention steps, and one method that sounds useful but isn't proven

The practical fix extension offices recommend is simple but easy to skip when you're in a hurry before a freeze: bare-root the plant, rinse the soil off the roots over a strainer or cloth (and dispose of that rinse water and debris rather than pouring it back into another bed), and repot into fresh, bagged potting mix rather than scooped garden soil. If you want to screen soil you're unsure about, a mustard-powder drench (roughly a third of a cup of ground mustard per gallon of water, poured over a small area) is a legitimate way to force any worms present to the surface so you can collect and discard them — but treat it strictly as a detection tool. A University of Vermont Extension specialist has said there's no scientific data showing mustard drenches actually control a jumping worm population, so it's not a substitute for starting with clean soil in the first place.

None of this means fall transplanting is a bad idea — moving tender plants indoors before a hard freeze is exactly the right call, and it's a call apartment growers have had to make on a near-weekly basis this month. The only change worth making is swapping the soil at the same time: bare-root divisions before they come inside, rinse off the old soil, and start them in fresh bagged mix rather than whatever was in the ground outside. It's a five-minute step that costs nothing extra beyond a bag of potting soil you probably needed anyway, and it's the difference between bringing a plant indoors and potentially bringing an infestation indoors with it.
