On October 6, 2026, the U.S. Energy Information Administration published its 2026–27 Winter Fuels Outlook, and the electricity numbers in it are the ones that matter most to anyone running a grow light, a seedling heat mat, or a humidifier indoors all winter rather than heating with gas or oil. EIA projects residential electricity prices rising about 3% nationally this winter, and because homes that heat mainly with electricity are also expected to use slightly more of it, their bills are forecast to rise about 4%, averaging roughly $1,196 for the season. That's not evenly spread: the Northeast is actually projected down about 3%, the Midwest and South up about 3% each, and the West up 9% — the largest regional increase in the report. Underneath the retail number, EIA says wholesale power across the hubs it tracks is forecast to average $52 per megawatt-hour in 2026, up 11% from 2025, with the PJM region (mid-Atlantic into the Midwest) the outlier at 41% higher wholesale prices this year before an expected pullback in 2027. None of that is a reason to panic, but it is a reason to stop guessing what a grow setup actually costs to run this winter, because 'about 3-4% more than last year, more if you're in the West or PJM footprint' is now a specific, citable number instead of a feeling.

Why this year's number is worth checking instead of assuming it's the same as last winter

EIA's outlook is explicitly a base case built on the assumption that this winter's temperatures look like last winter's — it gets revised through the season as part of EIA's monthly Short-Term Energy Outlook, so the 3-4% figures aren't guaranteed, they're a starting estimate. What makes them worth paying attention to now rather than shrugging off as 'rates always creep up' is the size of the gap between regions: a renter in the West is looking at a 9% jump in electricity outlays this winter, nearly three times the national average, while the PJM wholesale market (serving roughly a dozen mid-Atlantic and Midwest states) saw wholesale prices jump 41% this year specifically. If you're an apartment grower in either of those footprints running a grow light on a timer for 12-16 hours a day, a heat mat under a seed tray, and maybe a humidifier to fight dry heating-season air, you're adding load on top of a rate that's already moving more than the national headline suggests. Checking your own utility's current residential rate against what you paid a year ago, rather than relying on the national average, is the first real step — EIA publishes state-level detail in the same outlook for exactly this reason.

Before changing anything, measure what your own setup actually draws

The grow-light and heat-mat guides that estimate 'running cost' from a bulb's rated wattage are doing math on a number that's often wrong in practice — rated wattage is a ceiling, not what the device pulls at the wall, and a thermostatically-controlled heat mat in particular cycles on and off rather than drawing its rated wattage continuously. A smart plug with actual energy monitoring, like TP-Link's Kasa line (the KP115 and newer Matter-compatible KP125M), logs real watts and kWh per device through the app, lets you enter your own per-kWh rate for a dollar estimate, and breaks usage down by day, week, and month — so instead of estimating what a grow light or heat mat costs this winter, you get your own device's actual number on your own current rate. That matters more this year than most, because a setup that cost, say, $4 a month last December could cost noticeably more now depending on which EIA region you're in, and the only way to know without guessing is to measure it directly rather than trust a generic wattage-times-hours formula.

If what you're actually lighting is a few seedlings or a houseplant shelf, you're probably running more wattage than the job needs

A lot of apartment grow setups default to a full-size panel rated in the hundreds of watts because that's what gets recommended for serious vegetable production, when the actual load is a windowsill of seedlings or a shelf of houseplants that don't need anywhere near that output. GE sells a 9-watt LED grow bulb line (sold in both A19 and BR30 shapes, aimed at either fruiting/flowering plants or seeds and greens depending on the variant) that's a genuine low-draw option for that smaller job — worth noting that GE's own listings aren't fully consistent on lumens and PPF between the two variants, so check the specific model's spec sheet rather than assuming one number applies to both. On the heat-mat side, the energy waste usually isn't the mat itself — it's running one without a digital thermostat controller, which cycles power on and off once the root zone hits the set temperature instead of leaving the mat powered continuously; one manufacturer's own manual notes that if a properly-matched mat and controller takes more than five hours to reach the set point, that's usually a sign of a setup problem (poor insulation, a misplaced probe) rather than normal operation, not a reason to just let it run unmonitored all winter.

Nothing in EIA's outlook is specific to grow lights or houseplants — it's a national residential electricity forecast — but the regional spread in it is exactly the kind of thing generic apartment-gardening advice glosses over. A 3-4% national bill increase is a rounding error; a 9% jump in the West or facing a 41%-higher wholesale market under PJM is not, and which one applies to you depends entirely on where you live, not on how big your plant setup is. The actual response isn't to panic or to rip out a grow light — it's to replace assumption with a number: measure what your specific setup draws with an energy-monitoring plug, downsize the lighting wattage to match what you're actually growing instead of what a full-tent guide recommends, and make sure a heat mat is cycling off a thermostat rather than running unmonitored. None of that depends on EIA's forecast turning out exactly right this winter — it's just the set of moves that make your actual bill, whatever it ends up being, smaller than it would be otherwise.
