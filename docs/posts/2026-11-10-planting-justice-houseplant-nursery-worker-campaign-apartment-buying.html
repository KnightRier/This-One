The Houseplant on Your Windowsill Probably Came From One of Five Florida Nurseries — What the 'Planting Justice' Campaign Means for How You Buy Your Next One

WeCount!, a South Florida immigrant workers' rights group, put out a new update this week on its year-long 'Planting Justice' campaign, which is aimed at the company that grows more of America's houseplants than anyone else: Costa Farms. The campaign's own numbers are the part apartment growers don't usually think about — WeCount says roughly 70% of the indoor foliage sold in the U.S. is grown in South Florida, and a survey it ran of more than 300 nursery workers there found 86% had gotten sick on the job, mostly from heat and pesticide exposure. The campaign, modeled on the produce industry's Fair Food Program, is asking Costa Farms and the big retailers that sell its plants — Target, Home Depot, Lowe's, Trader Joe's, Aldi, Sprouts, Costco, IKEA — to sign onto a worker-written code of conduct with independent monitoring. As of this week, Costa Farms has told CBS News Miami that 'worker safety is a priority' but hasn't publicly committed to the code, and none of the named retailers has announced an agreement. None of that changes what's sitting on a nursery shelf today, but it does change the smartest way to buy your next houseplant.

The scale here is the actual news: one company grows a lot of what's in your apartment

Costa Farms isn't a niche supplier — it's the largest houseplant grower in the country, operating nurseries in five states, and it's the name most of the major retail chains are sourcing from whether or not the pot has a brand tag on it. That concentration is why WeCount built its campaign around one company instead of the whole industry: pressure one of the biggest growers into an enforceable, independently-monitored code, and the standard has leverage across a huge share of retail shelf space. The campaign's report, 'The Human Cost of Houseplants,' is based on worker surveys rather than anonymous claims, and multi-state actions this fall targeted Costa Farms locations directly, including a petition drop at its Leicester, NC nursery and another at its Apopka, FL headquarters. That's worth knowing not to boycott a specific brand — most pots don't say where they came from — but because it explains why 'where did this plant actually come from' is a harder question than it should be for something you're about to put on a windowsill for the next five years.

Until this resolves, a local grower is the only source that can actually answer the question

A big-box plant display can't tell you which nursery grew a given tray, and a mail-order listing usually can't either — the supply chain is exactly the kind of multi-state, multi-grower system WeCount is pushing back on. An independently-owned plant shop or a grower at a local farmers' market is the one source that can actually name their supplier, or tell you they grow it themselves, because they're not buying from a distribution warehouse. That's a reasonable thing to ask before paying full price for a new specimen, especially for a bigger-ticket plant you're planning to keep for years. It won't settle the labor dispute, but it does mean your money is traceable to an answer instead of a shrug.

Propagating your own cutting sidesteps the sourcing question entirely

The other practical move, and the one that scales better for an apartment grower who adds plants regularly, is to get more of your collection from cuttings rather than new retail purchases — a cutting from a friend's pothos, monstera, or tradescantia carries no nursery supply chain at all. A proper propagation setup makes this reliable enough to actually rely on instead of a windowsill glass of water that half the time doesn't root: a dedicated propagation station with multiple vessels, a rooting hormone to speed and improve root development on woodier stem cuttings, and a small humidity dome for cuttings that are slow or fussy to root. None of this requires giving up new plants entirely, but every cutting that roots is one less purchase where the sourcing question even comes up.

Nothing about the Planting Justice campaign changes overnight — Costa Farms hasn't signed onto the code of conduct WeCount is asking for, and the retailers named in the campaign haven't announced terms either, so this is a live labor dispute, not a settled outcome. What it does do is put a number on something apartment growers rarely think about: a huge share of what's sold as a houseplant in this country traces back to a small handful of Florida nurseries, with one company dominating that supply. Until that resolves one way or another, the two moves that actually put a buyer's money somewhere accountable are the same ones that happen to be good apartment-gardening practice anyway — buying from a grower who can answer where a plant came from, and growing more of your collection from cuttings you already have access to.
