A huge share of balcony and patio containers down here are things that have never once seen a hard freeze — citrus, bougainvillea, tropical hibiscus, bird of paradise, elephant ears — and most renters in Baton Rouge, Lafayette, Hattiesburg, or Beaumont simply don't own frost cloth or a plant dolly because they've never needed one before this week.

Why this freeze is a bigger deal for Gulf South balconies than for anyone further north

A 31-33°F low for a few hours is background noise to a Minneapolis balcony garden — everything on it is already dormant or indoors. It's a different problem in Baton Rouge, New Orleans's suburbs, or coastal Mississippi, where the growing season barely pauses and the plants people keep on patios and balconies skew tropical and subtropical: potted citrus, bougainvillea, hibiscus, elephant ears, bird of paradise, even bananas. None of those have meaningful cold tolerance — citrus can show leaf damage in the low 30s and real wood damage below 28°F, and tropicals like elephant ears and bananas can lose their above-ground growth outright at a hard freeze. Because this kind of warning is rare here, most apartment and condo growers in the warning zone don't own the gear a northern grower already has on hand: frost cloth, a soil thermometer, or even a plan for where a four-foot citrus tree in a pot actually goes when it can't go in a garage. The forecast window is short — a few hours before dawn — but short doesn't mean survivable without doing anything.

What to actually do tonight versus what can wait

Move what you can move: anything in a pot under about 15 inches across — hibiscus, smaller bougainvillea, elephant ear divisions — comes inside, into a garage, or at minimum tight against an exterior wall under a covered patio where it's shielded from radiational cooling. For anything too large or too heavy to move, water the soil before the cold arrives (moist soil holds heat better than dry soil overnight) and cover the whole plant down to the ground with frost cloth or, in a pinch, a sheet or moving blanket — the point is trapping the heat radiating up from the ground, not insulating the plant from the air. Don't use plastic sheeting directly against foliage; it conducts cold and can do more damage than no cover at all where it touches leaves. Uncover everything once temperatures climb back above freezing in the morning, since trapped humidity under a cover in full sun is its own problem. Given that WBRZ's own forecasters expect this to repeat Saturday night, this isn't a one-off scramble — anyone in the warning or watch zones is going to want frost cloth and a plan in place for the rest of the weekend, not just tonight.
