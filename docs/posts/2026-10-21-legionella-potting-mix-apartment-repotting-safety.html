If you're one of the apartment growers tearing open a fresh bag of potting mix this week to pot up everything you're bringing in before the cold hits, it's worth two minutes before you do. Western Australia's Department of Health put out a renewed warning on August 31, 2026 after 82 Legionnaires' disease notifications over the prior three years tied to Legionella longbeachae in potting mix and compost — 42 people hospitalized, two deaths. New Zealand health officials followed on October 5 with their own reminder: 152 legionellosis cases notified nationwide between September 2025 and August 2026, 54 of them in the South Island alone, 46 of those hospitalized. Both warnings are framed around spring planting season there, but the bacterium itself isn't seasonal or regional — it lives naturally in bagged potting mix sold everywhere, including in the US, and the actual risk factor is dust inhalation in an enclosed space. That's the part that matters here: a lot of advice assumes you're kneeling in a yard with the bag open to open air. Repotting on a kitchen counter or at a coffee table, in a room with the windows shut because it's 45 degrees outside, is a meaningfully different exposure than working outdoors.

What's actually in the bag, and why indoor repotting changes the risk

Legionella longbeachae is a different species from the Legionella that causes outbreaks in cooling towers and hot tubs, but it causes the same disease — Legionnaires', a severe form of pneumonia — and it gets into your lungs the same way: by inhaling contaminated dust or fine aerosols, not by touch. It's a normal, common inhabitant of commercial potting mix and compost; Consumer NZ's lab testing of 20 retail potting mix products found the bacteria present across brands, and its scientists were explicit that there's no brand that's reliably free of it — this isn't a defective-batch recall situation, it's a baseline property of the product category. The symptoms (fever, chills, cough, shortness of breath, muscle aches, appearing 2-10 days after exposure) mimic flu closely enough that gardening-related cases get missed unless the person specifically mentions handling potting mix to their doctor. Older adults, smokers, and anyone immunocompromised are the highest-risk group, but healthy adults have been hospitalized too. The mechanism that matters for apartment dwellers specifically: opening and mixing a dry bag releases fine particulate into the air, and in a small, closed room with no cross-breeze, that dust has nowhere to go and nothing diluting it before you breathe it in — the opposite of the open-air conditions most official guidance implicitly assumes.

The handling routine health agencies are actually recommending

The guidance across Western Australia Health, Consumer NZ, and Ireland's Health Protection Surveillance Centre gardener checklist is consistent on specifics: don't shake or punch the bag open, since that's exactly what aerosolizes dust — cut it open at arm's length with scissors or a utility knife instead. Dampen the mix with a little water before you start scooping or mixing it into a container; wet material doesn't become airborne the way dry mix does. Wear gloves while handling it, and wash your hands immediately afterward regardless — gloves protect against hand-to-mouth contact, not inhalation. And work somewhere with real airflow: an open window with a cross-breeze, a box fan pointed outward, or, if you have any outdoor access at all, a balcony or stairwell landing rather than a closed kitchen. None of this requires new gear beyond the mask and gloves, but it does mean treating a bag of potting mix with slightly more care than most apartment growers currently do, especially mid-fall when everyone's doing a round of repotting at once ahead of the heating season.

If you don't have a balcony: contain the mess instead of fighting the ventilation

Not every apartment has outdoor access, and cracking a window wide open in late October in a lot of the country isn't realistic. The practical fallback is a foldable repotting mat or tray set up right under the open window with a fan running, so the dust has a defined path out rather than settling across the room — it also means you're not vacuuming potting mix dust out of carpet and upholstery afterward, which just re-aerosolizes some of what you were trying to avoid inhaling in the first place. Do the actual bag-opening and dry mixing step there, with the mask on; once the mix is damp and in the pot, the active dust-generating part of the job is over and the rest is normal repotting.
