For an apartment, it's usually a space constraint dressed up as an aesthetic: a 6-7 foot cut tree doesn't fit a studio, and a cut tree also means a week of dropped needles on a floor you can't vacuum until the whole household is asleep. The option that gets buried a few bullet points down most of these roundups is the one that actually solves both problems at once — a live, potted Norfolk Island pine, which looks like a Christmas tree, survives the season as a houseplant afterward, and is already stocked at Costco, Target, and Walmart under the Costa Farms label.

Why a Norfolk Island pine works where a cut tree can't

A Norfolk Island pine (Araucaria heterophylla) isn't a true pine or fir — it's a houseplant with soft, feathery, symmetrical branches that happen to read as 'Christmas tree' once you add lights. Apartment Therapy and multiple houseplant guides call it out specifically as a small-space alternative to a cut tree because it stays small enough for a tabletop or a console for years, never needs a tree stand or a skirt to hide dropped needles, and goes right back to being a normal houseplant in your living room on January 2nd instead of going out to the curb. Costa Farms sells it pre-decorated in sizes as small as about 16-18 inches tall including the pot, which is the size category Costco and Walmart are currently carrying it in for the holidays.

The tradeoff nobody mentions: it's a tropical plant, not a conifer

The catch, which several care guides flag and most gift-shop displays don't, is that a Norfolk Island pine is genuinely tropical and has none of a real conifer's cold tolerance — it needs to stay away from drafty windows, cold entryways, and exterior doors in winter, which is exactly where a lot of apartment dwellers would otherwise put a 'Christmas tree' to show it off from the hallway. It also wants bright, indirect light to hold its shape; in a north-facing apartment or a unit with small windows, the lower branches tend to thin out and droop over a few months, which apartment-plant writers specifically warn about. And its branches are genuinely weak compared to a fir's — heavy glass ornaments or a string of old incandescent lights will visibly sag the tips, so this is a tree for light, small ornaments, not a family collection of ceramic heirlooms.

If you don't want a long-term houseplant commitment

Not everyone wants a Christmas tree that's still a maintenance obligation in March.
