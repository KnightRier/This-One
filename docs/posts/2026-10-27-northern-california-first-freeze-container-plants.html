The National Weather Service office in Eureka issued a Freeze Warning the night of October 23 into the morning of October 24 for northern Lake County and the northwestern and northeastern interior of Mendocino County, with lows forecast down to 29°F — plus a separate Frost Advisory for the Mendocino coast and southwestern interior, including Comptche, Philo, and Boonville, down to about 35°F, with patchy fog expected to form overnight in the Anderson Valley. Follow-on frost advisories kept getting issued for parts of inland Northern California through the rest of that week. None of that is dramatic by Midwest or Northeast standards, where growers plan around a hard freeze every October. But it's a genuinely different situation for apartment and condo balcony growers in the North Coast and wine-country interior, where winters are mild enough that many people have never once covered a container plant before this week. If you've spent the whole growing season just leaving pots on the balcony and this is your first freeze warning, the gaps that trip people up aren't about whether to cover — it's about what "covering" actually has to include for something growing in a pot rather than in the ground.

A covered plant in an uncovered pot still freezes from the bottom

The advice to "cover your plants" that circulates every time one of these warnings goes out is written mostly for in-ground gardens, where the soil mass around the roots holds enough stored heat overnight to buffer a few degrees of frost. A container doesn't have that buffer — the root ball is small, surrounded on all sides by air instead of earth, and sitting above grade on a balcony or patio where cold air pools. Draping a sheet over the foliage while leaving the pot itself exposed protects the leaves but does nothing for the roots, which are usually the part that actually dies in a first freeze like this one. For anyone used to a climate where this has simply never come up, the one new habit worth building this week is wrapping the pot itself, not just the plant growing out of it.

Wrap the container, then weight the cover down to the ground

Because the pot wall itself conducts cold straight to the root zone, an insulated wrap around the container — bubble wrap, burlap, or a purpose-made pot sleeve — does more for a first-time freeze than a heavier blanket on the foliage alone. Whatever cover goes over the top also needs to reach all the way down to the soil or deck surface and get weighted or pinned there; cold air pooling underneath an unsealed cover defeats most of the benefit, which is an easy mistake to make the first time you're doing this and just drape fabric loosely over a plant like you'd throw a blanket over furniture.

The official forecast is for a zone, not your specific balcony

A Freeze Warning or Frost Advisory is issued for an NWS forecast zone covering a wide area, but balconies and patios in that same zone can run several degrees colder or warmer than the official reading depending on exposure, elevation, and how sheltered the spot is from wind — which matters more than usual the first time you're deciding whether a 35°F advisory actually threatens your specific plants. A cheap outdoor min/max thermometer left where your containers actually sit, checked the next morning, tells you what your balcony really saw overnight rather than what the valley floor or airport sensor the warning was based on recorded. That's worth setting up now, before the next advisory, rather than guessing retroactively after a plant shows damage.

Outdoor min/max thermometer

A basic analog or digital thermometer that records the lowest temperature reached overnight, letting a balcony grower check their own microclimate against the official advisory instead of assuming the two match.
