Your Grow-Light Timer Has Been Drifting All Month — November 1's Time Change Is About to Make It Worse

If you set a plug-in timer for your indoor grow lights or hydroponic kit back in September and haven't touched it since, it's already wrong. Daylight is shrinking by roughly a minute and a half to two minutes every single day in October — in the Jacksonville area, for example, sunset moves from about 7:11 p.m. on October 1 to 6:39 p.m. by October 31, a 52-minute shift in one month, and northern-tier states lose closer to an hour and a half of daylight over the same stretch. A timer set to a fixed clock time doesn't know any of that happened; it just keeps firing at the same minute it always has, so the gap between when your lights actually come on and when the room actually goes dim keeps growing all month. Then, on Sunday, November 1, 2026, at 2 a.m., Daylight Saving Time ends and clocks fall back an hour on top of that — turning a schedule that was already drifting into one that's off by nearly two hours relative to actual daylight if nobody resets it.

Why a timer that was right in September is already wrong now

A basic plug-in timer, the kind most people set once and forget, only knows clock time — it has no idea when the sun actually sets in your location today versus three weeks ago. Because October's daylight loss is roughly linear and relatively fast (about 1-2 minutes a day depending on latitude, more the farther north you are), a schedule dialed in on October 1 to turn supplemental lights on right as natural window light fades is, by Halloween, kicking in nearly an hour late relative to what your plants are actually experiencing — they're sitting in the dark for that gap every evening. For anything relying on a consistent photoperiod — flowering houseplants, herbs, seedlings — that creeping mismatch is the kind of thing that shows up weeks later as leggy growth or stalled flowering with no obvious cause, because nobody thinks to blame a timer that "hasn't changed."

November 1 adds a full hour on top of that drift

When clocks fall back at 2 a.m. on November 1, 2026, the sun will start setting an hour earlier by the clock than it did the day before — on top of whatever gradual October drift already built up. A fixed-time timer left untouched doesn't adjust for this at all; a basic digital or mechanical outlet timer needs to be manually reprogrammed that same day, or your grow-light schedule is instantly an hour (or, combined with the October drift, close to two hours) out of sync with real daylight. Once the timing is fixed, recalibrate the duration too

Fixing when your lights turn on only solves half of it — how long they stay on matters just as much once natural light keeps shrinking through November and December. Iowa State University Extension's guidance for supplemental lighting is to set the timer to switch on as natural light fades and run for about 5 to 6 additional hours, which for plants getting little or no outdoor light works out to a total of 12 to 16 hours of light per day to keep them actively growing rather than just surviving. If you're using an app-controlled smart plug instead of a standalone timer box, look for one with a built-in sunrise/sunset trigger rather than only a fixed custom-time option — TP-Link's Kasa app, for instance, lets a schedule trigger off "Sunset" with an offset instead of a specific clock time, which is synced to your location and sidesteps the whole drift problem without needing a dedicated astronomical timer at all.
