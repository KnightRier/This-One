Check your actual light level before you assume you need anything

The two numbers printed on grow light boxes — watts and lumens — are both poor measures of what a plant needs. Watts just measure the electricity a bulb draws, not the light it puts out, and lumens measure brightness the way the human eye perceives it, which skips over the red and blue wavelengths plants actually use for photosynthesis. University of Minnesota Extension's own conversion is that 1 foot-candle equals 10.76 lumens, and their rule of thumb for indoor plants is roughly 7,000 to 7,500 lumens per square foot for strong growth, or as low as 2,000 lumens per square foot for plants with minimal light needs. Rather than guess which bucket your apartment windows fall into post-equinox, a cheap handheld lux or PPFD meter gives you a real reading in the exact spot you're planning to put a plant, which tells you whether you need a small supplemental clip light or a full setup before you spend money either way.

If you do need supplemental light, buy for distance and duration, not wattage

Once you know your space is genuinely short on light, the two settings that matter more than the wattage on the box are distance from the plant and hours per day. UMN Extension recommends keeping seedlings 4 to 6 inches from the light source and raising the fixture as they grow, since even LED and fluorescent bulbs that run cooler than old incandescent or high-pressure sodium bulbs still lose intensity fast over distance. On duration, extension guidance for supplemental fall and winter lighting is typically just 3-4 hours of added light after natural daylight fades, layered on top of whatever a plant is still getting from the window — not 12-16 hours of lights doing all the work from scratch. A grow light with a built-in timer removes the most common failure mode here, which is forgetting to turn the light off and stressing plants that also need a real dark period.
