The Gnats Show Up Two Weeks Later: Treating a Fungus Gnat Outbreak After You've Already Brought Plants Indoors

If you moved balcony or fire-escape plants back inside earlier this month and did everything right — neem spray, a few days' quarantine, a close inspection — you can still end up with a cloud of tiny flies around your pots by early October. That's because fungus gnats complete an egg-to-adult cycle in about three to four weeks at normal room temperature (65-75°F), so eggs already sitting in the soil before your plant ever came inside have time to hatch, feed, pupate, and emerge as adults well after the move is done and the pre-treatment spray has worn off. A single adult-focused fix won't catch that timeline. Here's the actual three-part protocol, and why skipping any one part just resets the clock.

The swarm you see isn't the problem — the larvae in the soil are

Adult fungus gnats are harmless nuisance flies; the actual damage (and the next generation) comes from larvae feeding in the top inch or two of wet soil for 10-14 days before pupating. Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis (BTI), sold as granules you water into the soil, is the standard fix because it targets gnat and mosquito larvae specifically and is inert to pets, people, and beneficial insects. It kills larvae within 24-48 hours of the granules being watered in, but it does nothing to eggs that haven't hatched yet, which is the detail that trips people up when they apply it once and declare victory.

Sticky traps don't kill the infestation, but they tell you when you're winning

Yellow sticky traps only catch adults — they can't touch eggs or larvae in the soil — so they're not a standalone fix. Their real value is as a monitoring tool: place a few traps at soil level across your pots, and a falling daily catch count over 2-3 weeks is the clearest signal that the larvicide and drench are actually working. Plastic-board traps with a waterproof adhesive hold up better than paper-backed ones in the higher humidity that comes with grouped houseplants, since damp paper traps lose stickiness and sag.

A hydrogen peroxide drench closes the gap BTI leaves open

Mix 1 part standard 3% hydrogen peroxide with 4 parts water (about 1/4 cup peroxide to 1 cup water per plant) and water it directly into the soil until it runs out the drainage holes. It fizzes on contact with organic matter and kills larvae and some eggs on contact, but the effect is contact-only and temporary, so the standard protocol is to repeat it at every watering for 2-3 weeks — not a single application — because new eggs continue hatching every 4-6 days from adults that were already flying before treatment started. Letting the top inch of soil dry out fully between waterings during this window also matters: gnat eggs and larvae can't survive in dry soil, so pairing the drench with a genuinely drier watering schedule (rather than your normal routine) is what actually shortens the 3-4 week cycle instead of just running alongside it.

None of these three steps alone breaks the cycle: BTI kills existing larvae but not eggs, sticky traps catch adults but not what's in the soil, and a one-time peroxide drench misses whatever hatches next week. Run all three together, let the soil surface dry between waterings, and expect it to take the full 3-4 weeks the gnat life cycle actually runs on — not the few days a single spray promises. If you already did a pre-move neem treatment and gnats still showed up, that's not a sign it failed; it just means eggs were already in the soil before the plant ever crossed the threshold, and this is the follow-up protocol for that exact situation.
