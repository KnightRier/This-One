The CDC Just Closed a 16-State Sprout Outbreak — The Next Day, Two More Alfalfa Recalls Surfaced

On November 9, the CDC declared an end to the E. coli and Salmonella outbreak tied to alfalfa sprouts that had sickened at least 77 people across 16 states (6 hospitalized, no deaths), which FDA traced to a single contaminated seed lot. That would normally be the tidy ending to a story this site already covered when the recalls were active in October. Instead, within a day or two, two more alfalfa-sprout recalls turned up in FDA's records — Country Creek LLC's "Country Creek Acres" alfalfa sprout seeds, and Francisco Family Foods' hydroponically grown alfalfa sprouts sold only in Montana — and neither one has been confirmed as connected to the outbreak that just closed. Marler Blog's framing of it is the right one for anyone who sprouts seed on an apartment windowsill: two sprout outbreaks are over; the seed problem isn't.

This week's two recalls, and why neither one made headlines

Country Creek LLC recalled its Country Creek Acres alfalfa sprout seeds (FDA Enforcement Report event #100206) after its own seed supplier issued a recall upstream; one food-safety outlet reports roughly 200 pounds involved, though FDA's own enforcement entry doesn't yet list a confirmed quantity, initiation date, or distribution area, and it isn't established whether this seed lot is linked to the outbreak that just closed. Separately, Francisco Family Foods of Kalispell, Montana (event #100236) recalled its own hydroponically grown alfalfa sprouts — sold in 4-oz zip-style bags and 1-lb gusseted bags, retail distribution limited to Montana — for possible E. coli and Salmonella contamination. What's unusual, as Food Safety News specifically flagged, is that both recalls surfaced only in FDA's routine weekly Enforcement Report rather than a press release or a company recall notice: no announcement, no retailer alert email, just an entry in a government database that most people never check.

The seed-lot problem doesn't go away when the outbreak closes

Two separate alfalfa-sprout outbreaks closed out in 2026, and in both cases the FDA traced the contamination to seed itself, not to anything that happened after sprouting began — which is the structural issue with jar sprouting that no amount of careful rinsing fixes. A contaminated seed coat goes into the jar already carrying the problem; the warm, consistently moist days of sprouting just give it ideal conditions to multiply. For an apartment grower who wants home-grown greens without re-litigating that risk every few weeks, soil-grown microgreens are a meaningfully different category: a thin layer of growing medium instead of a submerged, repeatedly-rinsed jar, and a much shorter, better-ventilated grow cycle. It's not a food-safety guarantee, but it sidesteps the specific seed-in-standing-water mechanism behind this entire string of 2026 recalls.

Still sprouting alfalfa in a jar? Check FDA's Enforcement Report directly, not just the news

Because this week's two recalls never generated a headline, following recall news alone means you could miss them for weeks. FDA publishes its Enforcement Report every Wednesday, searchable by firm name and product category, and it's where both of this week's alfalfa recalls actually live. It's worth the thirty seconds to search "alfalfa" or "sprout" there before you buy fresh seed or keep using what's already open on the counter — treat it the same way you'd check a VIN against a vehicle recall rather than waiting for a news story to tell you.

Nothing about this week's news means alfalfa sprouting is newly dangerous — it means the specific risk (contaminated seed, not contaminated technique) never fully went away just because one outbreak got officially closed. If you're still jar-sprouting, label and date what you start, and spend thirty seconds on FDA's Enforcement Report before you restock.
