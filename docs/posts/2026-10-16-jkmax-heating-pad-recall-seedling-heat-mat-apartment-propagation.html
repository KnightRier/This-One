A 195,000-Unit Heating Pad Recall Is a Good Reason to Stop Warming Seed Trays and Cuttings With a Bedding Heating Pad

The Consumer Product Safety Commission announced recall 26-778 on September 17, warning that JKMAX heated blankets and heating pads — sold only on Amazon.com from September 2022 through July 2026, in a one-pack or two-pack, under model numbers JKMAX-002, JKMAX-7284B, JKMAX-6284B, JKMAX-10090B and JKMAX-8490B — can overheat because of a defect in the internal wire heating elements. JKMAX has logged 577 reports of units smoking, melting, or burning, including 53 burn injuries, out of roughly 195,000 units sold; the fix is to stop using them and contact JKMAX for a full refund. This is a bedding recall, not a plant-gear recall, but it's worth flagging here because the exact product category it covers — a cheap electric pad bought for warmth — is also what a lot of apartment growers reach for under seed trays and cutting domes instead of buying something actually built for that job, and the way propagation setups get used is a worse-case scenario for the hazard the CPSC just described.

Why a seed tray or cutting dome is a harder environment for a heating pad than a bed

A bedding heating pad is designed around a person being on top of it, awake enough to notice if something smells like it's smoking, and generally not leaving it running continuously for weeks unattended. A propagation setup is the opposite on every count: it typically runs 24/7 for the two to four weeks it takes seeds to germinate or cuttings to root, it's frequently covered by a clear humidity dome that traps heat against whatever is producing it instead of letting it dissipate into open air, and it sits directly under trays and soil that are kept consistently moist — right where the CPSC says this specific defect (overheating internal wire elements) causes smoking, melting, and burning. None of that is unique to the recalled JKMAX units; it's a mismatch between what any bedding heating pad is built and rated for and how apartment growers tend to actually use one. A mat built for horticultural use is rated for continuous, covered, damp operation in the first place.

The thermostat is what actually keeps it from overheating, not the mat

A bare heat mat (and a repurposed bedding pad even more so) has no feedback loop — it just runs at whatever output it's built for regardless of how warm the room already is or whether a humidity dome is trapping extra heat on top of it. The standard fix in propagation guides is a separate thermostat controller with a probe you bury in the actual seed-starting medium or near the base of the cuttings, set to hold a specific temperature (commonly cited around 75-80°F for cuttings) rather than just leaving the mat on. The controller cuts power once the probe reads the target temperature and turns it back on once it drops, which is the mechanism a plain heating pad — recalled or not — simply doesn't have.

If anything heat-related is running unattended in your apartment, put it on a protected outlet

Most apartment propagation shelves — a closet, a spare corner, under a desk — aren't on a GFCI-protected circuit the way a kitchen or bathroom outlet is, even though the setup often involves water, a timer, and days of unattended operation while you're at work. A portable GFCI outlet adapter between the wall and whatever you're running (heat mat, humidity dome fan, grow light) adds the same ground-fault protection a bathroom outlet has, cutting power if there's a short — a cheap backstop for exactly the kind of electrical fault the CPSC says is behind this recall's overheating reports.
