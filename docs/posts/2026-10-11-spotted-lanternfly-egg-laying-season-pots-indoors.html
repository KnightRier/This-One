What you're actually looking for, and why it's easy to miss

Spotted lanternfly egg masses look like a smear of gray putty, dried mud, or plaster — not like an insect at all, which is exactly why extension entomologists keep emphasizing how often people walk right past them. They run about an inch to an inch and a half long, show up on any smooth, hard, flat-ish surface, and females deliberately choose things that aren't trees: pot rims, the undersides of plastic saucers, ceramic glaze, outdoor furniture legs, fence rails, and the walls of storage bins are all fair game. Penn State Extension's guidance is blunt about the stakes: an egg mass left in place is viable from roughly October through July, meaning anything you scrape onto the ground and leave there can still hatch next spring. Before a pot comes through your door this week, run your hand and eyes over the rim, the saucer (top and bottom), and the base — not just the visible side facing the room.

Scraping isn't enough on its own — the eggs have to actually contact alcohol

Multiple state extension sources, including Connecticut's entomologists quoted this week, are explicit that knocking an egg mass to the ground doesn't kill it — the eggs can still hatch in soil or mulch. The method that actually works is scraping the mass directly into a container of rubbing alcohol or hand sanitizer and leaving it submerged for at least 24 hours before disposal. For an apartment grower doing this on a balcony or in a hallway, that means having a small dedicated container on hand before you start moving pots, not improvising with whatever's under the sink once you've already found one.

The range is still spreading, so 'not my region' is getting less reliable every season

The Boston Globe's September 7 reporting on nearly 150 affected Massachusetts communities — about three times the prior year's count — plus a likely first wild New Hampshire population, is the clearest sign that this isn't staying confined to Pennsylvania and New Jersey. Pennsylvania's own quarantine zone, which restricts moving plants, outdoor furniture, and other articles that could harbor the pest, still sits at 56 counties after last year's expansion. For apartment growers, the practical upshot is less about where you live right now and more about where any newly bought plant or secondhand outdoor furniture came from — a detail worth asking about if you're buying a potted plant from a big-box store or online retailer that ships out of an affected region before you add it to a collection that's about to move indoors for winter.
