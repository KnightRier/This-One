Why the Next Bag of Potting Mix You Buy Might Not Be Mostly Peat Anymore — What That Means for Repotting in an Apartment

Horticultural trade press has been reporting a genuinely historic squeeze on the raw materials that go into bagged potting soil this year. The Dutch substrate and potting-soil trade association VPN has described the current peat and coir supply as the tightest since 1998, after a bad 2025 harvest in the Baltic states — the main source of the white sphagnum peat used in growing media — left yields at only about 40-55% of normal volumes, with existing stockpiles nearly exhausted while demand keeps climbing, driven in part by China. Separately, coir (coconut-fiber) suppliers have reported rising procurement costs and raw-material availability concerns since the start of 2026. None of that is apartment-specific — it's commercial greenhouse-supply reporting — but it matters to anyone repotting houseplants this fall, because growers facing the same squeeze are already shifting recipes toward perlite, bark, wood fiber, and compost to stretch what peat and coir they have. That means the bag of "potting mix" on the shelf this repotting season may not be the same recipe as last year's, which is worth knowing before you dump a new bag into a pot you can't easily redo in a small space.

Check the ingredient list, not just the brand, before you repot

Because the shortage is pushing growing-media producers toward substitute ingredients, the honest move for apartment growers is to actually read the back of the bag instead of assuming this year's mix behaves like last year's from the same brand. A mix leaning more on bark and wood fiber generally drains faster and holds less water than a peat-heavy one, which changes your watering interval — something that matters more in a small space where you likely have just one or two pot sizes and no room to experiment with a failed repot. If a new bag feels noticeably coarser or lighter than what you're used to, water a little sooner the first few times and watch the plant rather than sticking to your old schedule on autopilot.

Several mainstream potting-soil lines now sell an explicitly peat-free or peat-reduced formulation (bark/compost/wood-fiber based) alongside their traditional mix, which is the more supply-stable option this season and worth comparing side by side with the standard bag.

Coir bricks are the apartment-friendly way to hedge against the squeeze

If coir availability is also tightening, buying a compressed coir brick now rather than waiting on bagged mix has a real small-space advantage regardless of the supply story: a dry brick takes up a fraction of the storage space of an equivalent bag of ready-mixed soil, which matters when your only storage is a closet shelf. To use one, submerge the brick in water (reviewers report roughly five minutes for full saturation) until it expands, then break it up — a dense brick can be hard to divide by hand, and some growers resort to cutting an unused portion off with a saw before rehydrating the rest. Coir alone has almost no nutrient content and drains less freely than you'd want for most houseplants, so plan to cut it with perlite rather than potting it straight.

A single dry brick expands into several liters of growing medium and stores flat and compact — useful for repotting a few houseplants without keeping a bulky open bag of soil in a small apartment. Cut unused portions off dry before rehydrating, since a saturated brick is hard to split.

Feed the plant separately if you go coir- or compost-heavy

Peat is nutritionally inert too, but growers are used to buying peat-based mixes pre-loaded with a starter fertilizer charge; coir and bark-heavy substitute blends don't reliably carry the same feed, and some coir brick listings that claim added nutrients are unverified seller claims rather than a guaranteed feed charge. The practical fix is to not assume any substitute medium is feeding your plant and instead put a houseplant-appropriate fertilizer on a schedule from the start, especially for anything you just repotted into a coir- or bark-leaning mix going into lower-light winter months when the plant can least afford a nutrient gap.

A general-purpose liquid feed lets you start fertilizing on schedule immediately after repotting into a coir- or substitute-heavy mix, rather than assuming the bag or brick already carries a nutrient charge the way traditional peat-based mixes often do.

The peat and coir supply crunch reported this year is a commercial-greenhouse story, not a houseplant one, but it reaches your repotting bench the same way any raw-material shortage reaches a shelf product: through a changed recipe. Before you repot this fall, actually look at what's in the bag rather than trusting the label from memory, consider a compact coir brick if you want a supply-chain hedge that also happens to suit small apartment storage, and feed deliberately rather than assuming a substitute medium carries the nutrient charge a traditional peat mix would. None of that requires panic-buying soil — it just means paying attention to a bag you'd otherwise grab without a second look.
