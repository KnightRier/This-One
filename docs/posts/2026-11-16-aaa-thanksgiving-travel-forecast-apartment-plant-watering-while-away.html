AAA's Thanksgiving travel forecast came out this week, and the number is almost as big as last year's record: 81.5 million Americans are expected to travel 50 miles or more from home over the holiday period, Nov. 24 through Nov. 30 — just shy of 2025's 81.6 million, which ends a three-year streak of record-breaking Thanksgiving travel. Nearly 90% of that, about 72.9 million people, is going by car, with another 5.96 million flying and roughly 2.6 million taking a bus, train, or cruise (up 4.1% from last year, per AAA's release). For anyone covering the holiday-travel beat, the story is about airports and gas prices. For apartment growers, the real story is the calendar: a seven-day window where nobody is home to check on a balcony or windowsill setup. A garden bed can coast through a week of neglect on stored soil moisture. A 6-inch plastic nursery pot, sitting in a heated apartment with the thermostat drying the air the whole time you're gone, usually can't.

Why a week away hits container plants harder than it sounds

The math that makes this a bigger deal for apartment growers than for anyone with a yard is simple: pot volume. A full garden bed holds a reservoir of soil and groundwater that a plant can draw on for weeks. A container holds maybe a few quarts to a couple gallons, and that's the entire water supply until someone refills it. Indoors, the problem compounds — most people leave the heat running (or set it to come on automatically) while they're away specifically to keep pipes from freezing over Thanksgiving week, and running heat is exactly what drops indoor humidity and speeds up how fast potting mix dries out. A plant that normally needs water every 4-5 days can be bone dry by day 3 of a heated, empty apartment, which means a straight 7-night trip (Nov. 24-30 is actually 6 nights, but plenty of people are extending it into a full week with travel days on either end) can mean two full missed waterings for anything in a small pot on a sunny sill.

For a handful of plants: terracotta watering stakes, no power or timer needed

If you're only trying to cover a half-dozen pots, the simplest fix is also the oldest: a gravity-fed terracotta stake pushed into the soil, connected by a tube to an inverted water bottle or jug. The unglazed clay tip stays slightly porous, so it only releases water as the surrounding soil actually dries out — it won't dump the whole reservoir in one go the way a loose wick can. Blumat's classic stakes are the version that keeps coming up in plant-care roundups as the most reliable of this style, largely because the clay cone is calibrated to regulate flow on its own rather than just dripping on a fixed schedule. Set it up a day or two before you leave so you can confirm it's actually drawing water, not just sitting in the pot looking busy.

For a full windowsill or balcony collection: a timer-driven drip kit

Past 6-8 pots, gravity stakes get fiddly to manage — that's the point where a small battery-powered drip irrigation kit earns its cost. These kits run thin tubing from a bucket or jug reservoir to individual drip emitters at each pot, controlled by a timer that fires on a schedule (typically once or twice a day) rather than relying on soil moisture alone. RAINPOINT's indoor/balcony kits are built around exactly this apartment-scale use case — sized for a cluster of 10-15 pots off a single reservoir rather than a whole yard — and the dual-knob or app-scheduled timers mean you're not guessing whether the schedule is actually running while you're at your in-laws'. Fill the reservoir, test the cycle once before you leave, and the setup keeps working on its own long after Thanksgiving week, through any other trip the rest of winter.

None of this requires buying gear for a trip you're taking once a year — the stakes and drip kits both get reused for every future long weekend, and they're worth setting up this week regardless of which 81.5 million-person travel crush you're part of. The one step that actually matters more than which product you pick: test whatever system you choose a day or two before you leave, not the morning you walk out the door. A stake that isn't actually drawing water, or a timer that didn't save its schedule, is something you want to catch while you can still fix it — not something you find out about when you get back from a week away to a windowsill full of crispy leaves.
