The Floral Marketing Fund's third wave of its Consumer Houseplant Purchasing Report, covered this past month by Greenhouse Grower, GCN, and Scripps News, found that U.S. houseplant buyers are shopping less often than during the pandemic years but spending more per trip, own more plants overall, and expect to spend more across all 10 plant categories the study has tracked since 2019. Trade coverage is framing this as a shift the whole industry has to adjust to. For anyone growing in an apartment, it's not a shift at all — it's just the national market catching up to a constraint small-space growers have been working around for years: when you don't have room for a dozen plants, every purchase has to be the right one, because there's no spare windowsill to absorb a mistake.

The data point that matters for a small space: buyers are betting bigger on fewer plants

Trade coverage of the Floral Marketing Fund study, including a Greenhouse Grower write-up and a GCN summary, both describe the same pattern: fewer store trips, more money per trip, and rising spending intentions across every plant category the fund has surveyed in 2019, 2021, and now 2025. Greenhouse Grower's follow-up piece by Nick Alan, founder of online houseplant retailer Palma Botanical, makes the apartment-relevant point explicit — a buyer who isn't purchasing often feels the loss of a plant more sharply, so they're reading care information more closely before they commit. In a studio or one-bedroom where a plant has to earn a specific spot in specific light, that's the same math apartment growers already do by necessity. The practical upshot from the coverage: before you buy, actually measure the light where the plant will live instead of eyeballing it, since a listing's "bright indirect light" claim is only useful if you know what your window delivers.

What the industry says separates a good online plant listing from a bad one

Because mail-order and online plant shops are a normal buying channel for apartment dwellers without a nearby garden center, the checklist in Nick Alan's Greenhouse Grower piece doubles as a buyer's checklist, not just a seller's one. He flags that pricier, less-frequent buyers now expect photos of the actual plant being shipped (not a generic stock photo), specific instead of generic care labels — he calls out sellers who tag every single plant "easy" or "low light" regardless of the species — and explicit language about pest inspection before shipping. That last point matters more in an apartment than almost anywhere else: plants in a small space usually sit close together on the same shelf or windowsill, so a pest problem on one new arrival (spider mites, fungus gnats, scale) has a much shorter distance to travel to everything else you own.

None of this is apartment-specific advice from the Floral Marketing Fund's researchers — it's a national retail trend the trade press is covering as news to growers everywhere. But the behavior it describes (fewer purchases, more money and attention per plant, closer reading of light and care claims, more weight put on pest-free shipping) is exactly how small-space growers already have to shop, because there's no room to buy wrong five times and let the failures go unnoticed in a backyard. If the rest of the market is finally buying the way an apartment always had to, the practical move is to lean into it: measure your light before you buy, read listings for specifics instead of generic "easy plant" labels, check new arrivals for pests before they sit next to anything else you own, and spend a little more on the few tools that protect a purchase you're not making often.
