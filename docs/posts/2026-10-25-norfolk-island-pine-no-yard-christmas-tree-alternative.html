A Norfolk Island pine is a real, if imperfect, answer to both: it's sold small enough to live on a side table, and because it's a houseplant rather than a cut conifer, it doesn't die on you in January — it just goes back to being a plant.

It isn't a pine, and that's exactly why it survives on a windowsill instead of a curb

Despite the name, Norfolk Island pine (Araucaria heterophylla) isn't a true pine at all — it's a subtropical conifer native to a tiny Pacific island with no winter dormancy requirement, which is the whole reason it tolerates being a permanent houseplant instead of something that has to go back outside. That same subtropical origin is also its one real vulnerability: Missouri Botanical Garden and University of Missouri Extension both note it's sensitive to cold and needs to be kept away from drafts and sudden temperature swings, and florist-industry chilling-injury guidance for holiday plants generally puts the danger zone below 50°F — relevant right now because this is exactly the season these show up pre-decorated at big-box stores, often carried out to a car in the first real cold snap of the year. The fix is simple and the same one that applies to every tropical plant bought outdoors this time of year: don't let it sit in a cold car or an unheated trunk, and get it straight from checkout into a warm room rather than running other errands first.

Pick the size for the room you actually have, not the display model at the store

Home Depot's current Costa Farms lineup runs from a 4-inch pot (around 10 inches tall, roughly $27) up through 6-inch pots (1-2 ft tall) to 10-inch decor pots shipped at an average 3 feet tall and priced near $59-79 when pre-decorated with bows or candy-cane trim. For an apartment, the realistic choice is almost always the 6-inch or 10-inch size on a side table or plant stand, not the 3-footer, which needs floor space and enough overhead clearance that its soft, horizontally-tiered branches don't get crushed against a ceiling or shelf. Growth is slow — a few inches a year indoors — so whatever size you buy this winter is roughly the size you'll have next winter too, which makes it worth sizing for the room rather than for how full it looks at the store.

Decorate light — the branches are the one thing that doesn't forgive heavy ornaments

Multiple plant and holiday-décor guides agree on the same point: Norfolk Island pine branches are soft and considerably more brittle than real pine or fir, so standard glass ornaments and strung incandescent lights are the wrong call — the weight bends or snaps the tiered branch structure, and it doesn't grow back the way a cut tree's needles regrow next year, because this is a plant you're keeping. The decorating products actually built for small/tabletop trees already assume this: battery-powered miniature LED light sets (commonly 2-3 AA batteries, no cord to find an outlet for) and lightweight mini ornament sets sized for 1-2 ft trees are sold specifically for small artificial tabletop trees, but the same low-weight logic is exactly what a live Norfolk Island pine needs. Skip the tinsel and garland entirely — both trap humidity against the foliage and can encourage the same fungal issues any houseplant gets from being damp and crowded.

The appeal here isn't that a Norfolk Island pine looks exactly like a Christmas tree — it doesn't, and it won't smell like one either. It's that it sidesteps the two things a cut tree does worst in an apartment: it never has to be dragged down a hallway and out to a curb that may not take trees at all, and it doesn't force a one-month-only commitment of floor space you don't have to spare. Buy it protected from the cold on the way home, size it to the table or floor space you actually have rather than the display model, and decorate it the way small tabletop trees are already built to be decorated — battery-powered lights, foam ornaments, no tinsel. Do that and the plant you bring home this week for the holidays is still a perfectly normal, permanent houseplant come February.
