A New 50% Tariff on Canadian Plant Goods Just Hit the Exact Things Apartment Growers Buy: Moss, Cuttings, and Bulbs

2026-10-09

Disclosure: this page contains affiliate links. If you buy through them, Small Space Grow may earn a small commission at no extra cost to you. We only recommend products based on our own research.

On August 22, 2026, the U.S. imposed a 50% tariff on a wide range of Canadian goods under Section 338 of the Tariff Act — and unlike most trade actions in recent years, it was written to apply even to shipments that would otherwise qualify for duty-free treatment under the USMCA/CUSMA trade agreement. Multiple trade-law firms and horticulture trade groups (McMillan LLP, Blakes, Thompson Hine, and Landscape Ontario) have confirmed the product list includes dormant bulbs and other planting material, unrooted cuttings and slips, mosses and lichens, certain ornamental foliage, and certain seeds — Canada responded with its own matching counter-tariffs on September 8. None of that sounds like apartment-plant news, but those exact categories are what a surprising amount of the indoor-growing supply chain is built on: packaged sphagnum moss, rare houseplant cuttings sold by specialty growers, and imported spring bulbs. Here's what's actually affected and what to buy instead while landed costs work their way through to retail shelves.

Packaged sphagnum moss (orchids, LECA, propagation stations) is on the list by name

"Mosses and lichens" appears explicitly in the Section 338 product coverage reported by Landscape Ontario and Global News, and Canada is far from a minor player here — AmericanHort has told Congress that Canadian operations supply roughly 85% of North America's sphagnum output, with Canada accounting for about 96% of U.S. sphagnum peat moss imports. That dependency is mostly about peat moss used in bagged potting soil, but dried long-fiber sphagnum — the kind apartment growers pack around orchid roots, line LECA claybeds with, or use to start propagation stations for pothos and philodendron cuttings — comes from some of the same Canadian suppliers, and a 50% import surcharge on a Canadian-origin product doesn't stay with the importer; it shows up at the register. The fix isn't complicated: check the bag before you buy. Not all sphagnum sold in the U.S. is Canadian-sourced.

Bagged potting mix: the peat-vs-coir math just got more lopsided

Peat-based potting soil was already a target of a separate, longer-running tariff fight — AmericanHort has specifically lobbied for an exemption or a reduced "critical mineral" rate for Canadian sphagnum peat moss, precisely because the U.S. has so little domestic supply to fall back on. Whatever the final rate lands at, the direction of travel is the same: a product nearly all of it coming from one tariffed country is a bad bet for stable pricing. For apartment-scale repotting, where you're buying one bag a year rather than a greenhouse's worth, coconut coir is a straightforward substitute — it's a byproduct of the coconut industry, sourced mostly from South and Southeast Asia, and has nothing to do with the Canada tariff situation at all.

Rare cuttings and imported bulbs: check the seller's country before you buy, not after

"Unrooted cuttings and slips" and "dormant bulbs" are both named categories on the Section 338 list, which matters a lot to anyone buying rare aroid cuttings or spring-flowering bulbs through specialty sellers rather than big-box stores — a meaningful share of the rare-houseplant cutting trade (Monstera, Philodendron, Anthurium variants) runs through small Canadian growers selling via Etsy and specialty nursery sites, and a U.S. buyer importing one now has a 50% surcharge built into the landed cost whether the listing price has caught up yet or not. Spring bulbs are a mixed bag: Longfield Gardens' own buying guide notes that most of the amaryllis and tulip bulbs sold in the U.S. come from the Netherlands, Brazil, Peru, South Africa, and Israel — not Canada — so a bulb order from a Dutch-grower-sourced retailer is largely unaffected. The practical move for both: check the grower's listed location before adding to cart, and lean on propagating what you already own instead of importing new starter material.

Nothing about this tariff makes sphagnum moss, potting soil, rare cuttings, or spring bulbs unavailable — it makes the Canadian-sourced version of each one about 50% more expensive to import, with the cost landing on whoever brings it into the U.S. first and then, eventually, on the shelf price. For an apartment grower buying in small quantities, the fix is less about panic-buying and more about checking labels: a bag of sphagnum or potting mix that lists a non-Canadian origin, a bulb order built on Dutch rather than Canadian stock, and a bottle of rooting hormone to multiply cuttings you already have all sidestep the surcharge entirely. Watch for price increases on Canadian-labeled products over the next few billing cycles as retailers work through pre-tariff inventory — that's when the 50% actually shows up at checkout, not necessarily today.
