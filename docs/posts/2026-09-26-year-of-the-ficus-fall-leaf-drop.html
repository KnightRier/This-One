The National Garden Bureau named 2026 the Year of the Ficus, and the genus — fiddle leaf figs, rubber plants (Ficus elastica), weeping figs (Ficus benjamina), Ficus Audrey — has been showing up in apartments all year as a result. That timing is unlucky. Late September is also when a lot of apartment growers are dragging a ficus in from a balcony or bright patio spot ahead of cooling nights, or watching the post-equinox drop in window light hit a plant that hasn't moved at all. Clemson HGIC's factsheet on weeping fig is blunt about what happens next: ficus react to almost any stress — a move, a repot, a draft, over- or under-watering, low light — by dropping leaves, sometimes dramatically. A plant a lot of people bought into this year's trend without knowing that is about to test their patience.

The leaf drop is the plant's default reaction, not a sign you killed it

Per Clemson HGIC, weeping figs 'seem to react to almost any stress by shedding leaves,' with overwatering, underwatering, drafts, low light, and being moved or repotted all listed as common triggers — and NC State Extension's plant toolbox entry for Ficus benjamina adds that it is 'very sensitive to light,' so a move alone can trigger leaf loss as the plant adjusts and regrows foliage suited to its new spot. The extension guidance is also the reassuring part: if the plant's actual growing conditions are adequate, it stops dropping leaves and pushes healthy new growth on its own — the leaf drop is the adjustment, not a diagnosis. The practical move if you just brought one in from outside or off a brighter windowsill is to pick one stable spot with bright indirect light and leave it alone for several weeks rather than chasing the leaf drop by moving the pot around, which only restarts the stress response. A humidity gauge for that spot is worth the money before you do anything else, since Ficus benjamina wants at least 40% humidity and apartment heating season air is often well below that.

Don't 'fix' the leaf drop by watering more — that's the overcorrection that actually kills it

University of Maine Cooperative Extension's ficus guidance is specific about the seasonal switch: keep soil evenly moist during active summer growth, but reduce watering once growth slows into fall and winter, and skip fertilizer entirely during that low-growth period. The common mistake extension sources flag is the opposite instinct — seeing leaves drop and responding by watering more to 'revive' the plant, which on a ficus that's already stressed by a move or lower light is more likely to cause root rot than to help, since Clemson HGIC notes soggy, slow-draining soil is itself a leading cause of the same leaf-drop symptom. A cheap soil moisture probe removes the guesswork: check a few inches down before watering rather than watering on a fixed schedule, especially through the exact weeks a moved or trend-bought ficus is adjusting.

Check for the pests it may have brought in from outside before you blame the move

Clemson HGIC's weeping fig factsheet lists spider mites and scale insects as the most frequent ficus pests, with mealybugs, aphids, and thrips also common — and any of these can cause leaf yellowing or drop that looks identical to ordinary transition stress. University of Maine Extension's practical tip is to wipe down leaves (or run the plant through a lukewarm shower) every few weeks, both to remove dust that blocks light absorption and to get a close look at leaf undersides, which is where scale and spider mites are usually first visible. This matters specifically for a ficus that just came in from a balcony or patio, since that's exactly how outdoor pests hitch a ride indoors for the winter — checking now, before symptoms are bad enough to spread to other houseplants nearby, is cheaper than treating an infestation later.

The Year of the Ficus designation is a real trend, not just marketing — but it means a lot of apartments now hold a plant genus that Clemson HGIC describes as reacting to 'almost any stress' with leaf drop, right at the point in the year when moves indoors, cooling nights, and post-equinox light drops are all happening at once. If your ficus is dropping leaves right now, the extension guidance is consistent: pick one stable, bright, indirect-light spot and stop moving it, cut back watering rather than increase it, check the leaf undersides for the scale and spider mites it may have picked up outdoors, and give it a few weeks. Ficus punish inconsistency, and this is the season with the most of it.
