If you keep anything in pots on a balcony, patio, or fire escape in that corridor, the next 72 hours are about three specific, unglamorous jobs — not a plant emergency, but a logistics one.

Move or anchor anything tall or top-heavy before the wind watch starts

Gardening extension advice on windy balconies is consistent: short, squat, heavy containers hold up to gusts far better than tall, skinny ones, which topple easily once wind gets above the 40-50 mph range this storm is already producing along the Mid-Atlantic coast. If you can't move a pot fully indoors or into a stairwell, the next best option is pulling it away from railings and into a corner or against a wall where the building itself blocks some of the gust, and grouping smaller pots together so they brace each other rather than standing alone. The problem for apartment growers is usually just weight — a saturated 14-16 inch pot of potting mix is heavy enough that moving it solo isn't realistic, which is where a rolling plant caddy earns its cost: get pots that need to move onto one before Friday afternoon rain starts, not after the railing's already slick and the wind's already up.

Get pots up off the deck before the rain, not after

The forecast 2-6 inches of rain over three days is the bigger threat than the wind for most container plants, because a pot sitting flush against a balcony floor or patio slab can't drain freely even with drainage holes in the bottom — the water backs up underneath instead of running off. Extension and gardening guidance is blunt on the fix: raise the pot on feet, bricks, or risers so there's an air gap underneath, which lets excess water actually flow out instead of pooling and saturating the root zone from below. It's a five-minute job per pot now; it's a much longer conversation with a plant showing root rot symptoms in two weeks. If you use saucers under any pots, plan to empty them Saturday and Sunday between rain bands rather than letting them sit full through the whole system.

Don't resume your normal watering schedule once it clears Monday

University extension guidance on post-flood plant care is specific about the most common mistake: gardeners see soggy soil, wait for it to look dry on the surface, then go straight back to their usual watering routine — while the soil six inches down, where most of the root system actually sits, is still saturated. The recommended check is physical, not visual: probe down a few inches and only water again once it's actually dried out at root depth, since waterlogged pore spaces starve roots of oxygen and that's what triggers rot, not the rain event itself. Extension sources also recommend a damage pass once the storm clears Monday — check for broken stems, wind-stripped leaves, and any standing water that didn't drain, and prune back anything visibly damaged rather than leaving it to rot in place.
