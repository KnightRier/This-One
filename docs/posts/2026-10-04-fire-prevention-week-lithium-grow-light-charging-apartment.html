Fire Prevention Week runs October 4-10, 2026, and for the second year running the National Fire Protection Association built the whole campaign around one thing: lithium-ion battery charging. The official theme is "Charge into Fire Safety: Safe Charging Is a Superpower," and the core guidance — use the charger that came with the device, charge on a hard flat surface instead of a bed or couch, unplug once it's full, stop immediately if something feels hot or looks swollen, and buy only products carrying a recognized testing-lab mark — is usually illustrated with e-bikes and phones. Nobody mentions the grow light. But a small-space plant setup is one of the few corners of an apartment that's quietly accumulated several of exactly the devices this campaign is warning about: cordless clip and puck grow lights with built-in lithium batteries, USB power banks feeding them, and a timer-driven power strip underneath the whole shelf that nobody unplugs for months.

The grow light on your shelf is a lithium-ion device, not just a lamp

Battery-powered puck and clip grow lights have become the default fix for the one shelf in an apartment with no nearby outlet, and they work precisely because they hide a real lithium-ion cell behind a plastic housing. The bigger risk is usually the power strip, not the battery

Most apartment grow setups don't run on battery power at all — they run a plugged-in grow light, a humidifier, and sometimes a seedling heat mat off one timer and one power strip tucked behind a plant stand, often for the entire growing season without being unplugged or inspected. NFPA's "buy only listed products" guidance for lithium devices applies just as directly here: a power strip or smart plug without a UL or ETL mark is the same unverified-electronics risk as an uncertified charger, just running continuously instead of for a few hours. A UL-listed surge-protected strip rated for the actual combined wattage of a grow light plus a humidifier is the cheap, boring fix most grow shelves are missing, and it's worth checking the rating printed on whatever strip is already back there before adding one more device to it.

A five-minute check that covers everything already on the shelf

NFPA's single clearest warning sign for a lithium-ion device going bad is heat it shouldn't be producing, and the easiest way to catch that early on a crowded plant shelf is a non-contact infrared thermometer — point it at a grow light's battery housing, a USB charging brick, or the power strip itself after a few hours of normal use, and a reading that's climbing well above room temperature on a device that's supposed to be idle is the cue to stop using it and check the manufacturer's guidance, rather than waiting for a smell or visible swelling to notice something's wrong.

None of this is a reason to panic about a grow light — it's a reason to spend five minutes during the one week a year fire departments are actively pushing this message. Apartments pack an unusual density of small chargers and continuously-running power strips into one corner for the plant shelf, which is exactly the pattern NFPA's 2026 theme is aimed at: not primarily e-bikes, but the ordinary habit of charging something on a soft surface, walking away, and not checking the certification on the power strip underneath it.
