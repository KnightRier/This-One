Winter Electric Rates Are Jumping Across Most of the Country — Here's What That Does to Your Grow Light Bill

2026-10-18

A tracker maintained by the Center for American Progress and the Natural Resources Defense Council, last updated in early August, found that electric rates have already risen or are proposed to rise soon for 71% of U.S. electricity customers — more than 116 million people across 49 states and Washington, D.C. — with at least 275 utilities involved and roughly $78.9 billion in added electric costs projected by 2028. The increases already locked in for this winter are specific: Rhode Island's utility regulators approved a 16% jump in Rhode Island Energy's winter supply rate starting October 1, to 14.77 cents per kilowatt-hour, adding about $22 a month to the average bill over the six-month winter rate period. Connecticut's Eversource is set to raise its supply rate roughly 13% on January 1, to 12.64 cents per kilowatt-hour. Pennsylvania's Duquesne Light and West Penn Power raised their supply rates 10.6% and 6.1% respectively heading into this heating season. None of this is about plants specifically, but if you're running a grow light panel for 12-16 hours a day, a seedling heat mat, a humidifier, or a small hydroponic pump through an apartment winter, electricity is already one of the bigger hidden costs of growing indoors — and in a lot of the country, this is the winter it gets measurably more expensive.

Your grow setup's real electricity cost is easy to underestimate — and rate hikes widen the gap

Here's the kind of back-of-envelope math that rate hikes make worth doing: a 100-watt LED grow panel run 14 hours a day uses about 1.4 kilowatt-hours daily. At a nationwide average residential rate of roughly 17 cents per kilowatt-hour, that's about $0.24 a day, or a little over $7 a month — but at Rhode Island's new 14.77-cent winter supply rate, or Connecticut's incoming 12.64-cent rate, that climbs with the local increase, and it's before you add a heat mat, a humidifier, or a hydroponic pump running on their own separate schedules. The only way to know your actual number instead of a nationwide average is to measure it, and a cheap plug-in meter gives you the real wattage draw of each piece of equipment instead of trusting the box's rated maximum, which most devices rarely hit in practice.

A scheduled smart plug fixes the single most common waste: equipment left running with no schedule at all

The most common inefficiency in an apartment grow setup isn't the equipment itself, it's that none of it is on a timer. A grow light left on a manual power strip tends to run longer than its actual photoperiod because nobody's flipping it off exactly on time every day, and a seedling heat mat bought for germination is meant to come off once seedlings have true leaves, not stay plugged in for the rest of the season warming a windowsill that doesn't need it. If your light is still an older T5 fluorescent or CFL fixture, this is the rate environment where switching to LED pays back fastest

LED grow bulbs draw substantially fewer watts than T5 fluorescent tubes or CFL grow bulbs for comparable light output, which is exactly why the payback period on replacing an old fixture shortens whenever the per-kilowatt-hour rate goes up — the same wattage difference saves more money at 14.77 cents than it did at a lower rate last winter.
