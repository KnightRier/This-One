The 'Wild About Wildlife' category winner was a Norwich couple, Harriet and Stephen Festing, for a container-based balcony garden two floors up that hosts around 140 plant species and reportedly attracts bumblebees, butterflies, and even bats. Before you copy a 140-species balcony, work out what yours can actually hold

Balconies are engineered to a specific live-load rating, and that number is finite — it has to cover people standing on it, any furniture, and every container plus the soil and water in it, all at once. Gardening guidance that discusses this puts typical balcony load ratings in a rough range of about 30 to 50 pounds per square foot, with older buildings sometimes rated lower, and a commonly cited conservative fallback of around 20 pounds per square foot when you genuinely don't know your building's number. The part that catches people off guard is wet soil: saturated potting mix in a planter runs close to 100 pounds per cubic foot, meaning a single large planter after a good watering or a rainstorm can weigh far more than it did dry at the garden center. A handful of plastic pots can add up to real structural load faster than it looks. Before scaling up a balcony collection, check your lease or building documents for a stated load limit, or ask your property manager directly — for anything beyond a few modest pots, that's the same step the Festings took with their architect before they went further.

Keep the plant diversity, cut the weight: lightweight pots and mix instead of ceramic and garden soil

The same structural guidance that gives the load numbers above also points to the fix: lightweight plastic, resin, or fabric pots instead of ceramic, terracotta, or concrete planters, and a soilless, perlite- or coir-based potting mix instead of garden soil, which holds far more water weight per cubic foot. Positioning heavier pots near the balcony's load-bearing wall or corner rather than out at the railing edge is the other low-cost adjustment the same sources recommend, since that's typically where a balcony slab is best supported. None of this requires fewer plants — the Festings' 140-species result came from container diversity, not container size — it just means the containers holding that diversity should be chosen with weight in mind from the start, not after a load problem shows up.

The pollinator part is real too, and it scales down to a few rail planters

The wildlife draw in the Festings' garden — bumblebees, butterflies, and bats — isn't exclusive to a 140-species collection; it follows from nectar-rich flowering plants that bloom in succession through the season rather than all at once, which UK balcony-wildlife guidance (including the National Trust's own tips for the challenge) consistently points to over any single showy plant. Bats show up indirectly, drawn by the insects the flowers themselves attract after dark. For an apartment grower working with a rail planter or two rather than a full balcony, a pollinator-mix seed packet bred for container growing gets most of that effect without needing anywhere near 140 species — the mechanism is bloom diversity and timing, not sheer plant count.
