Check your seed bag against these exact recalls before you sprout anything this week

Gardeners Basics recalled its Waltham 29 Broccoli Sprouting Seeds on October 8, 2026 — specifically the 1-lb white-and-green resealable pouch, UPC 848137042889, marked 'Packed for 2027,' from bulk lot #W4105 after the seed supplier found Salmonella in a sample. It was sold online only: Amazon, the company's own site, TikTok Shop, Walmart, eBay, and Etsy. Eden Brothers (calabrese broccoli seed) and EAK Distribution's Home Grown green sprouting broccoli seed were recalled the same week from the same contaminated supplier lot. Separately, Todd's Seeds LLC (Cookeville, TN) recalled 37,481 units of alfalfa sprouting seed for possible Salmonella and E. coli, sold through Amazon and direct-to-consumer, with Dec. 31, 2029 printed expiration dates. None of these are a 'maybe stop using it' situation — the FDA guidance is explicit: don't eat the seed or anything you've already sprouted from it, and don't compost it either, since that doesn't reliably kill the bacteria. Seal it in a bag and put it in household trash, then contact the seller for a refund.

Sprouting is a genuinely higher-risk food category even without a recall

The reason sprout seed shows up in outbreaks more than most produce isn't bad luck — it's the growing method. Sprouts germinate in warm, consistently moist conditions for several days, which is also the ideal environment for any bacteria already on the seed coat to multiply, and California's Department of Public Health has flagged the seed itself as the most likely contamination source in most sprout-related outbreaks. A tray or open jar that doesn't drain and rinse completely leaves standing moisture between rinses, which is exactly the condition that lets contamination take hold. A proper sprouting jar with a stainless mesh lid — rinsed and fully drained twice a day, jar tilted at an angle so no water pools at the bottom — is a meaningfully better setup than a bowl or a damp paper towel method for an apartment counter.

Going forward, buy from a seller that actually publishes seed testing — not just a production date

None of this week's recalled seed was mislabeled or sold by a shady seller — Amazon, Walmart, and TikTok Shop all carried it. The practical takeaway isn't to stop sprouting, it's to pick a supplier that tests for pathogens as a stated practice rather than one that doesn't mention it at all. True Leaf Market, for example, publishes that it voluntarily tests its sprouting seed lots for Salmonella, E. coli, and Listeria (seed itself isn't FDA-regulated the way sprouts-for-sale are, so this testing is a choice, not a requirement) and retests for germination roughly every nine months. That's a self-reported practice, not an independent guarantee — it doesn't make a seed lot risk-free, and this week's recalls prove even established channels can carry contaminated stock. But a seller stating a testing process gives you something to point to, which a generic bulk-bin listing doesn't.
