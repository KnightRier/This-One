Cold kills roots slowly; wet snow breaks things fast

A hard freeze is a slow problem — it damages a plant over hours as cell water crystallizes. Several inches of wet snow sitting on a plant is a fast, mechanical problem: it just weighs the thing down until something gives. A trellis, a tomato cage, a leggy rosemary or a small citrus with a full canopy can snap a branch or tip a whole pot under a load that would be a non-event for a garden bed shrub rooted in the ground. In the KBSI and FOX2 coverage area (the band running toward Farmington, where the higher 4-10+ inch totals are forecast), that's enough wet snow to matter for anything top-heavy in a container. If you're leaving for Thanksgiving before this clears tonight or tomorrow morning, the move is to either lay anything with a full canopy on its side on the balcony floor (lower profile sheds less and can't tip) or bring it just inside the door, rather than leaving it standing upright and loaded.

The snow-to-rain switch is the part that actually threatens terracotta

Every forecast in this storm has the same shape: snow first, then a changeover to cold rain as temperatures climb through Thanksgiving Day. That transition is worse for unglazed clay pots than the snow itself. Snowmelt and rain soak into a porous terracotta wall, and if temperatures drop back below freezing afterward (which is realistic once the rain clears out behind the system), that trapped moisture expands and can crack or spall the pot — the same freeze-thaw cycle that ruins driveways and sidewalks, just happening to your planter instead. If you've got terracotta or ceramic containers in or near the path of this system, wrapping them now, before the first snow falls tonight, matters more than anything you'd do after the rain starts.

Burlap pot insulation wrap
