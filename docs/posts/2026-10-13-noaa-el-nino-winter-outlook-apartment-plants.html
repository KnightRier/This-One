NOAA Says This Could Be a Historically Strong El Niño Winter — Here's What That Means for Apartment Plants Depending on Where You Live

This isn't a short-term cold-front story — it's a full-winter one. NOAA's Climate Prediction Center has been ratcheting up its El Niño strength forecast through the fall, and the most recent ENSO diagnostic discussion (issued October 8) keeps an El Niño Advisory in effect with a greater-than-83% chance of a strong-to-very-strong event persisting through January-March 2027, with some read of the odds putting the chance of a historically strong event (stronger than any since 1950) above 80% for October-December alone — up from 69% back in August, according to coverage of CPC's updates. The seasonal outlook CPC issued on September 17, which is still the active winter outlook as of today, tilts the odds toward a wetter winter across the southern tier — Southern California and the Southwest, the Gulf Coast, and especially the Southeast — while favoring above-normal temperatures across much of the West and the northern tier, and leaning drier in the Pacific Northwest, the north-central U.S., and Hawaii. None of that is a guarantee for any single city, and CPC itself cautions that even a very strong El Niño doesn't tilt every location the same way — the strong 2015-16 event, for comparison, still ended up one of Los Angeles's driest years on record even though the earlier 1997-98 event dumped over 30 inches of rain there. But the broad shape of this winter is now well enough established that it's worth planning your indoor plant setup around it instead of just reacting week to week.

If you're in the wetter, cloudier zone (Southern California, the Southwest, Gulf Coast, Southeast): expect a dimmer winter at the window

CPC's forecast puts the highest odds of above-normal precipitation across the Southeast, with elevated odds extending from Southern California and the Southwest through Texas and the Gulf Coast. More rain in winter generally means more cloud cover, and for an apartment with plants depending on a single south- or west-facing window, a cloudier winter translates directly into fewer usable hours of real sunlight per day than a typical dry winter in the same spot would give you. That's a bigger deal for light-hungry plants — fiddle-leaf figs, citrus, succulents kept outside part of the year — than it is for low-light-tolerant pothos or snake plants. If you're in one of these regions and you've been getting by on window light alone, this is the winter to add a supplemental light rather than wait for visibly stretched or dropped leaves to tell you it wasn't enough.

If you're in the warmer, drier zone (Pacific Northwest, north-central U.S.): heating season is going to bite harder than usual

The same outlook leans drier for the Pacific Northwest and the north-central U.S., on top of the above-normal temperatures favored across much of the West and northern tier generally. Drier outdoor air plus a furnace or baseboard heat running to compensate for any cold snaps is the combination that strips humidity out of an apartment fastest — and tropical plants (calatheas, ferns, many aroids) show it first as browning leaf edges and crispy tips, often misread as a watering problem when it's actually an air-moisture problem. A cheap hygrometer sitting near your plants tells you the actual number instead of a guess, and most apartments running winter heat will read well under the 40-50% humidity these plants want.

The headline number — NOAA's October 8 ENSO update keeping the odds above 83% for a strong-to-very-strong El Niño running through early 2027, with a shot at historic strength — is real and worth knowing about before winter fully sets in, not after. What it means for your specific apartment depends heavily on where you live: a likely dimmer, wetter winter across the Southeast, Gulf Coast, Southern California and the Southwest argues for supplemental light; a likely drier, warmer-but-still-heated winter across the Pacific Northwest and north-central U.S. argues for watching humidity more closely than usual.
